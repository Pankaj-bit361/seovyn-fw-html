How to Fix a Flat Bike Tire: Tube and Tubeless Repairs

2026-10-03

A spare inner tube will not save a weekend ride if the glass that punctured the first tube remains in the tire. A tubeless flat needs a different approach: its sealant may close the hole without removing the wheel. This guide explains how to fix a flat bike tire, trace the leak, and decide whether the repair is safe to ride.

Move the bike off the road and identify whether the tire contains an inner tube. For a tube flat, remove the wheel and tube, clear the puncture cause, then patch or replace the tube and reseat the tire. For a tubeless flat, try sealant or a tire plug and confirm that the tire holds pressure. Stop if the casing, bead, or wheel is damaged.

Identify the setup and gather tools

Work somewhere stable and clear of traffic. A tubeless setup has a valve fixed to the rim and uses sealant inside the tire; a tube-type setup has a valve attached to an inner tube. A “tubeless ready” marking describes the tire’s capability, not proof that it currently has no tube. If the setup is uncertain, avoid pulling the bead off at the roadside.

Match the tools to the wheel:

Inner tubeTire levers, a correctly sized tube or patch kit, and a bike pumpRemove the tube and patch or replace it
TubelessA tubeless tire plug kit and bike pump; sealant in the tireLet sealant reach the hole, then plug it if needed

Check the tire’s size marking and whether the valve is Presta or Schrader before buying a replacement tube. Some axles also require a wrench. Cargo-bike and e-bike wheels can be difficult to remove; turn an e-bike off and follow its wheel-removal instructions. Stop rather than pulling on an unfamiliar motor cable or axle fitting.

How to fix a flat bike tire with an inner tube

A replacement tube is usually simpler to fit at the roadside than a patch. Keep the punctured tube until the cause has been identified: the hole’s position can point toward either the tread or the wheel rim.

  1. Remove the wheel and release all remaining air. Note how the axle is secured so the wheel can go back in correctly.
  2. Free one tire bead. Push the tire’s edge toward the rim’s center, then use a tire lever to lift that bead over the rim. Work around the wheel, leaving the other bead in place. Avoid a screwdriver or knife, which can damage the tire or tube.
  3. Remove and examine the tube. Pull out the tube and lift its valve from the rim. Add enough air to locate the leak by sound or feel; bubbles in water can reveal a small hole.
  4. Find and remove the cause. Align the tube with the tire to locate the corresponding spot. Michelin advises finding and removing the puncture cause before fitting a replacement tube. Inspect the tread and inside of the casing for glass, wire, or a thorn; remove debris carefully rather than sweeping bare fingers across it. Check the rim for sharp edges and confirm that the rim strip covers the spoke holes.
  5. Patch or replace the tube. Follow the patch kit’s preparation and application instructions. Give a replacement tube just enough air to hold its shape, put its valve straight through the rim, and tuck the tube inside the tire.
  6. Reseat and test. Work the bead back onto the rim by hand where possible. Partly inflate and inspect both sides for a trapped tube or uneven bead, then inflate within the applicable tire and rim limits. Reinstall the wheel securely; check that it turns freely and that the brake works.

A hole on the rim-facing side of the tube suggests a rim strip, spoke-hole, or rim-edge problem. Two nearby holes may indicate a pinch flat. Those clues make replacing the tube more than a temporary reset: they direct the rider to the part that needs attention.

How to fix a tubeless bike tire flat

Do not start by removing the tire. Rotate the wheel until the puncture is low enough for sealant to reach it, then inflate and see whether the leak stops. If sealant does not close a tread puncture, use a tubeless tire plug kit according to its instructions. Reinflate and check the plug and bead for escaping air; the same approach applies to a tubeless mountain bike tire.

A bead that has pulled away from the rim is a different problem from a hole in the tread. Inspect it for dirt or visible damage and check the valve and rim tape if they are accessible. If the bead will not reseat or a carried pump cannot reinflate the tire, stop the roadside attempt and arrange transport or shop help. Continuing to pump a tire that cannot make an airtight seal will not make a tread plug effective. Michelin’s tubeless-ready fitting guidance also calls for compatible rims, tape, valves, and sealant.

A slow leak with no clear tread puncture may be at the valve or bead instead. A torn sidewall, badly damaged casing, or plug that still leaks calls for assessment rather than another ride on a soft tire.

Patch, replace, or get help?

A correctly applied vulcanizing patch can be a durable tube repair, but it has no guaranteed number of rides. Park Tool describes pre-glued patches as temporary trailside fixes and vulcanizing patches as more durable. Replace a tube with valve damage or a hole that will not seal. Duct tape is not a dependable tube patch and cannot restore a cut tire casing.

Before setting off, make one final check:

  • Confirm that the puncturing object is gone and the rim strip remains over the spoke holes.
  • Check that the tire bead sits evenly, the wheel is secure, and the brake works.
  • Inflate the tire and confirm that it holds pressure before committing to the ride.

If the tire still loses air, or the wheel cannot be removed and reinstalled safely, do not ride on the rim. FAQ

Why did a new inner tube go flat in the same place?

Suppose the first tube has a hole facing the tread near the valve, and the replacement develops one in that position too. Align the valve with the rim hole, then inspect the matching part of the tire for a fine wire or glass shard still lodged in the casing. If both holes face the rim instead, inspect the rim strip and spoke holes before fitting a third tube.

How can a rider locate a slow tubeless leak?

Inflate the tire and apply soapy water around the valve, along both beads, and across the tread. Growing bubbles identify where air is escaping. Bubbles at the valve or bead point to a sealing problem rather than a tread puncture, so a plug in the tread will not address that leak. If the tire cannot retain enough air to test, arrange an inspection.

Can a tube be fitted to a tubeless-ready tire to get home?

A tubeless-ready tire can be mounted with an inner tube, as Michelin’s fitting guidance explains. First make sure the tire casing and bead are sound and the tube matches the tire size and valve opening. A tube will not make a torn casing safe. Fitting one may also be impractical at the roadside if the wheel cannot be removed safely.
